Bitcoin starts with a paper that appears in 2008, signed by someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto — a pseudonym nobody has ever been able to attach to a real identity. The paper is called *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*, and it's aimed at one specific problem: how do you stop someone from spending the same digital money twice if there's no bank or central authority keeping the official ledger?

The answer it proposes is a chain of blocks, each one linked to the last and ordered through proof of work. The point of the proof of work isn't just to order transactions — it's to make rewriting history expensive. If you wanted to alter something buried a few blocks back, you'd have to redo the costly work not just for that block but for every block that came after it, which quickly becomes impractical.

The idea didn't come out of nowhere — it grew out of the cypherpunk scene, building on earlier proposals like b-money and bit gold, both of which ran into the same wall: nobody had figured out how to get independent participants to agree on the order of transactions without a central party. Nakamoto's proof-of-work chain was the piece that finally solved that.

The system went from paper to reality on 3 January 2009, when the first block was mined. Embedded in it was the headline of a Times article about a bank bailout — a detail that does double duty: it proves the block wasn't created before that date, and it reads as a quiet comment on exactly the kind of financial system Bitcoin was built to route around.

New bitcoin enter circulation as a reward for mining, starting at 50 bitcoin per block and cutting in half every 210,000 blocks — roughly every four years. Because that reward keeps halving, the total supply converges on 21 million and will never exceed it.

The first real-world glimpse of bitcoin being used as money came in 2010, when someone paid 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas — now remembered as the first documented commercial transaction.

As for Nakamoto, the involvement ends in 2011, when they stop posting or participating publicly. The bitcoin associated with them from that early period have sat untouched ever since — never moved, never spent.
