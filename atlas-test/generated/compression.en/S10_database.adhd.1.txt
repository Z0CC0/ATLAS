For orders, catalogue, search and analytics together, the shape of the decision is set by orders: you need real transactions there, no negotiating. That already rules a few things out. ClickHouse is far ahead on analytics but weak on point updates, so it can't hold orders. MongoDB's multi-document transactions came late and are costly, so it's not a natural fit for orders either. MySQL is roughly equivalent to Postgres on orders but weaker on data types and complex queries. Postgres gives you real transactions and is solid enough — not brilliant, just solid — across all four workloads, which makes it the only reasonable place to put the authoritative data.

So start there, alone: Postgres for orders, catalogue, search and analytics. It won't be excellent at search or analytics, but it doesn't need to be yet. Its built-in text search is good enough under about a million documents and gets weak above that; its analytics hold up to tens of millions of rows before scans start dragging. Below those thresholds, adding anything else is just extra machinery for no real gain — adding Elasticsearch under a million documents, for instance, is almost always premature.

The catalogue is the one place a document store would make life easier, since MongoDB handles variable schemas more gracefully than Postgres does. But that convenience has to be weighed against the transaction cost mentioned above, and against not wanting a second authoritative store this early. Keep it in Postgres until the schema pain itself gives you a concrete reason to move.

When you do outgrow Postgres on a specific workload, the usual move isn't to replace it — it's to add a specialised system fed from it, with Postgres staying the source of truth. Elasticsearch is far better at search: relevance ranking, synonyms, typo tolerance are all things Postgres won't give you. But Elasticsearch isn't an authoritative store — it has to be fed from Postgres, never hold data on its own. Same logic for analytics: ClickHouse's columnar scans beat Postgres by orders of magnitude, but again, it's fed from Postgres, not a replacement for it.

Every system you add this way brings two costs that don't show up on a feature comparison: keeping it in sync with the source of truth, and keeping the data aligned between the two. Those costs are people's time, not licence fees — that's the actual price of "adding a specialised database," more than any technical merit of the system itself.

So the practical path: launch on Postgres alone for everything. Watch two numbers — document count for search, row count for analytics. Add Elasticsearch when search is demonstrably struggling past roughly a million documents. Add ClickHouse when analytics queries are demonstrably struggling past roughly tens of millions of rows. Don't add either on anticipation — add it when the workload demands it and you have the numbers in hand to justify it.
