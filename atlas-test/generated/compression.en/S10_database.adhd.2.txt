For a platform that has to handle orders, a catalogue, text search, and analytics, start with Postgres for everything and don't split the stack until you have a specific reason to.

The order data is the part that can't be sloppy — you need real transactions, and Postgres gives you that. MySQL would get you the same guarantee on orders, but it's weaker on data types and complex queries, so it doesn't buy you anything Postgres doesn't already have. MongoDB is tempting for the catalogue because your product schema will vary from item to item and that fits its model well, but multi-document transactions came late to Mongo and are still costly — not what you want anywhere near order processing. So the transactional core rules out MySQL and Mongo as the primary store; Postgres covers catalogue, orders, search, and analytics all from one place, adequately if not brilliantly at any of them.

That "adequately" is the part to watch. Postgres's built-in text search is fine while your catalogue or content stays under about a million documents; past that it gets weak, and relevance ranking, synonyms, and typo tolerance are areas where Elasticsearch is genuinely far ahead. Similarly, Postgres's analytics hold up to tens of millions of rows, but past that scans slow down, and ClickHouse's columnar engine is orders of magnitude faster for that kind of workload. So both of those are real upgrade paths — just not ones to take on day one.

The practical move is: run Postgres alone first, and only bring in Elasticsearch or ClickHouse when a specific workload is provably hitting its ceiling — actual numbers, not anticipation. Adding Elasticsearch before you're past roughly a million documents is almost always premature. If and when you do add either, keep Postgres as the single authoritative store and feed the other systems from it — Elasticsearch isn't meant to be authoritative on its own, and ClickHouse isn't meant for orders at all, since point updates are exactly what it's bad at.

The thing to budget for isn't the software cost of adding Elasticsearch or ClickHouse — it's the ongoing work of keeping them synchronized and aligned with Postgres. Every system you add is another pipe that can lag or drift, and someone has to own that. That operational cost, not licensing, is the real price of going beyond Postgres — which is exactly why the rule is to add a system only when a workload demands it with numbers to prove it, not preemptively.
