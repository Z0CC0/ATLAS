For a small project, go with Postgres.

The practical reason is that your jobs and your data can live in the same transaction. If you insert a row and enqueue a job in one transaction and that transaction rolls back, the job never fires — there's no window where a job runs against data that never actually got committed. That's a correctness guarantee you don't get for free with Redis.

You also don't have to think about durability. If the process crashes, the queue is sitting safely on disk in the same database you're already backing up — nothing extra to configure. Redis can do this too, but only if you remember to turn on AOF; skip that and a restart quietly drops whatever was queued.

Using `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` as your dequeue mechanism, Postgres comfortably handles up to about a thousand jobs a minute, which covers the vast majority of small projects.

The tradeoff: Redis is faster (sub-millisecond latency) and scales to much higher throughput. But it's another moving part — another service to deploy, monitor, and keep patched. For a small project, that operational cost usually isn't worth paying yet.

So: start with Postgres, and move to Redis later if your job volume actually grows past what a single-transaction queue can handle — not before.
