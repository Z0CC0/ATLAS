x86 went from a 16-bit chip to an interface that sits on top of a much more complex machine underneath.

1978, 8086: 16 bit. Grew out of 8080, compatible at source level.

1982, 80286: protected mode. No way back to real mode without a reboot.

1985, 80386: 32 bit, paged virtual memory, virtual 8086 mode.

1989, 486: first-level cache and maths coprocessor moved onto the chip.

1993, Pentium: superscalar. Two integer pipelines, up to two instructions retired per cycle.

1995, Pentium Pro: out-of-order execution. x86 instructions translated into internal micro-operations. From here x86 is an interface, no longer the real machine underneath.

2000, Pentium 4: chased frequency with a very long pipeline and hit the power wall.

2003, AMD64: AMD extended the architecture to 64 bit, and Intel adopted the same instruction set. First time the dominant extension did not come from Intel.

2006, Core 2: focus shifted from frequency to core count.

Across generations, vector extensions grew: MMX, SSE, AVX, AVX-512.

Today the complexity sits in the layers below: branch prediction, cache hierarchy, speculative execution.

2018, Spectre and Meltdown: showed speculative execution carried a security cost nobody had priced.
