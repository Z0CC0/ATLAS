Over 40 years, x86 went from a 16 bit chip to an instruction set that sits on top of a very different machine underneath.

1978, 8086: 16 bit, grew out of the 8080, compatible with it at source level.

1982, 80286: protected mode, but no way back to real mode without a reboot.

1985, 80386: 32 bit, paged virtual memory, virtual 8086 mode.

1989, 486: first level cache and maths coprocessor moved onto the chip.

1993, Pentium: superscalar. Two integer pipelines, up to two instructions retired per cycle.

1995, Pentium Pro: out of order execution. x86 instructions translated into internal micro-operations. **From here x86 is an interface, no longer the real machine underneath.**

2000, Pentium 4: chased frequency with a very long pipeline and hit the power wall.

2003, AMD64: AMD takes the architecture to 64 bit, and Intel adopts the same instruction set. First time the dominant extension did not come from Intel.

2006 onward, Core 2: focus moves from frequency to core count.

Across generations, vector extensions keep growing: MMX, SSE, AVX, AVX-512.

Today the complexity sits in the layers below: branch prediction, cache hierarchy, speculative execution.

2018, Spectre and Meltdown: **speculative execution turned out to carry a security cost nobody had priced.**
