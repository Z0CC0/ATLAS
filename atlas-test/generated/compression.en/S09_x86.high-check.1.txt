1978, 8086: 16-bit, grows out of 8080, source-compatible.

1982, 80286: protected mode arrives. No way back to real mode without reboot.

1985, 80386: 32-bit. Paged virtual memory. Virtual 8086 mode.

1989, 486: L1 cache and FPU move onto the chip.

1993, Pentium: superscalar. Two integer pipelines, two instructions retired per cycle.

1995, Pentium Pro: out-of-order execution. x86 instructions get translated into internal micro-ops. From here x86 is an interface, not the real machine underneath — the hardware runs something else entirely and just presents x86 at the boundary.

2000, Pentium 4: chases frequency, very long pipeline, hits the power wall.

2003: AMD extends x86 to 64-bit — AMD64. Intel adopts AMD's instruction set. First time the dominant extension didn't come from Intel.

2006, Core 2: direction shifts from frequency to core count.

Alongside all this, vector extensions keep growing: MMX, SSE, AVX, AVX-512.

Today the real complexity isn't the instruction set — it's the layers under it: branch prediction, cache hierarchy, speculative execution.

2018: Spectre and Meltdown show that speculative execution had a security cost nobody had priced in.
