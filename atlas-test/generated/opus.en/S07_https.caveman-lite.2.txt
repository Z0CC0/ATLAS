HTTPS is ordinary HTTP carried inside a channel that TLS encrypts. The web requests and responses are the same. The difference is the protected connection they travel through.

**Setting up the connection (the handshake)**

1. **Client hello.** Your browser opens the connection. It lists the TLS versions and ciphers it supports.
2. **Server hello.** The server picks one version and one cipher from that list. It also sends its certificate.
3. **Certificate check.** The certificate binds a domain name to a public key. A certificate authority signs it. Your browser checks that signature by following the chain of signatures up to a root certificate it already has. If the chain does not reach a trusted root, the check fails.
4. **Key agreement.** The client and server agree on a session key. Today they usually do this with an elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman exchange.

**Why a symmetric session key**

Asymmetric encryption, with its public and private keys, costs too much to use on real traffic. The two sides use it only during setup, then switch to a shared symmetric key for the actual data.

**After the handshake**

From this point, all traffic is encrypted and authenticated. Anyone in the middle can neither read it nor change it without being detected.

**TLS 1.2 vs TLS 1.3**

TLS 1.2 needs two round trips to finish the handshake. TLS 1.3 needs only one, so connections start faster.

**What the certificate does not tell you**

A valid certificate does not tell you who runs the site. It proves only that whoever controls the domain also controls that key. A padlock means your connection to that domain is secure. It does not mean the site is trustworthy.
