x86 went through three phases. From 1978 to 1995 each chip added features to the architecture. From 1995 on, x86 was a translation layer over a different machine. After the Pentium 4, the focus moved from clock speed to more cores, and later to the risks of speculative execution.

**Building the architecture (1978–1993)**

The 8086 of 1978 was 16-bit. It grew out of the 8080 and could run 8080 code after recompiling the source, not the binaries.

The 80286 of 1982 introduced protected mode. Once a program switched into it, the only way back to real mode was a reboot.

The 80386 of 1985 was the big step. It brought 32-bit computing, paged virtual memory and virtual 8086 mode, which let old real-mode programs run inside the new protected environment.

The 486 of 1989 put the first-level cache and the maths coprocessor on the same chip as the processor.

The Pentium of 1993 was superscalar. It had two integer pipelines and could finish two instructions per cycle.

**The break (1995)**

The Pentium Pro of 1995 introduced out-of-order execution. It also translated x86 instructions into internal micro-operations. From then on, x86 was an interface, not the real machine underneath. That idea explains everything that followed.

**Speed, 64-bit and cores (2000–2006)**

The Pentium 4 of 2000 chased clock speed with a very long pipeline and hit the power wall: faster chips were using too much power.

In 2003 AMD extended the architecture to 64-bit with AMD64, and Intel adopted the same instruction set. It was the first time the dominant extension to x86 did not come from Intel.

From 2006, starting with the Core 2, the direction changed from higher clock speeds to more cores.

Over the same generations, the vector extensions kept growing: MMX, then SSE, then AVX, then AVX-512.

**Today**

The complexity now sits in the layers below the instruction set: branch prediction, the cache hierarchy and speculative execution. In 2018, Spectre and Meltdown showed that speculative execution had carried a security cost nobody had priced.
