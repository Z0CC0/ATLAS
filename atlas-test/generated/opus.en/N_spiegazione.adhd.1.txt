## The short version

When you write data to a system that keeps several copies (replicas), those copies don't all update at the same moment. With eventual consistency, they disagree for a short while after a write. If writes stop, they all end up with the same data within a finite time.

## How long "eventually" takes

- **Replicas in the same region:** usually under a second.
- **Replicas in different regions:** it goes up to a few seconds.

## Why systems accept it

It's a trade against availability. Suppose replicas lose contact with each other. A system that insists on strong consistency must refuse requests until they reconnect. An eventually consistent system keeps serving requests and lets the replicas catch up later.

So eventual consistency isn't a bug to remove. It's a design choice, and most systems that scale make it on purpose.

## Why you should care

The person it hurts is whoever reads right after writing. For example:

1. A user posts a comment.
2. They reload the page.
3. The read goes to a replica that hasn't received the write yet, so the comment isn't there.

The user thinks the post failed. They may post again or decide your app is broken.

## The usual fix

Send the reads of the session that just wrote to the primary replica, which has the write. Everyone else keeps reading from any replica. The person who just wrote sees their own change, and the rest of your read traffic still gets spread across replicas.

## When it's not acceptable

There are only a few such cases, and they're easy to spot:

- **Account balances:** two replicas could each approve spending the same money.
- **Seat availability:** two people could book the same seat.
- **Unique identifiers:** two users could claim the same username or ID.

Use strong consistency for these. Eventual consistency is fine almost everywhere else.
