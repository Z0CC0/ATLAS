Over four decades x86 went from 16 bit to 64 bit, then from chasing clock speed to adding cores, and its complexity moved into the hardware beneath the instruction set.

8086, 1978: 16 bit, grew out of the 8080, compatible with it at source level.

80286, 1982: protected mode, but no way back to real mode without a reboot.

80386, 1985: 32 bit, paged virtual memory, virtual 8086 mode.

486, 1989: first-level cache and maths coprocessor moved onto the chip.

Pentium, 1993: superscalar. Two integer pipelines, able to retire two instructions per cycle.

Pentium Pro, 1995: out-of-order execution. x86 instructions translated into internal micro-operations. From here x86 is an interface, no longer the real machine underneath.

Pentium 4, 2000: chased frequency with a very long pipeline and hit the power wall.

AMD64, 2003: AMD extends the architecture to 64 bit, and Intel adopts the same instruction set. First time the dominant extension did not come from Intel.

Core 2, 2006: focus shifts from frequency to core count.

Vector extensions grew across generations: MMX, SSE, AVX, AVX-512.

Today most of the complexity is in the layers below the instruction set: branch prediction, cache hierarchy, speculative execution.

Spectre and Meltdown, 2018: showed that speculative execution carried a security cost nobody had priced.
