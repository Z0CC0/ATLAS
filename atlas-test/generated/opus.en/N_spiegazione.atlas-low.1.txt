Eventual consistency: after a write, replicas don't agree right away, but they converge within finite time once writes stop.

**Why it exists:** it's the price of availability. A system demanding strong consistency has to refuse requests whenever replicas can't talk to each other. Accepting eventual consistency keeps the system answering.

**Where you'll feel it:** reading right after writing. A user posts a comment, reloads, and doesn't see it. Convergence across replicas in the same region usually takes under a second. Across regions it rises to seconds, so that reload can easily land on a replica that's behind.

Common fix: send reads to the primary replica for the session that just wrote, not for everyone. That user sees their own comment, and everyone else keeps reading from any replica.

Why you should care: it's not a defect to eliminate. It's a choice, and most systems that scale make it deliberately. Your job is to spot the few cases where it's unacceptable. They're easy to recognise:

Account balances

Seat availability

Uniqueness of an identifier

Those need strong consistency. Nearly everything else can live with replicas catching up.
