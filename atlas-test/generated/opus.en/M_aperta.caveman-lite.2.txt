Before you choose microservices, a modular monolith or anything else, answer a few questions. The architecture should come from those answers.

**1. Find out which parts actually change.** Look at where changes land in the monolith. Stable parts gain nothing from being split out, so leave them alone. The parts that change often are the only ones where new boundaries might pay off.

**2. Map the transaction boundaries.** Check where your transactions start and end before you draw any service lines. A transaction that spans two services becomes a distributed problem. Keep each transaction inside one boundary.

**3. Don't do a big-bang rewrite.** Building a full replacement in parallel with the old system almost always fails. The old system keeps changing, and the copy never catches up. Use progressive strangling instead:
- Put a router in front of the monolith.
- Move one piece at a time behind the router.
- Keep the old system authoritative for each piece until the new version is verified.

**4. Count the operational cost of microservices.** Every service needs its own releases, monitoring, logs, alerts and a person who looks after it. With fewer than five developers, that cost almost always exceeds the benefit.

**5. Consider the modular monolith.** Few people suggest it, but it is often the right answer. You get sharp internal boundaries and a single release. You can still extract a module into its own service on the day you actually need to.

**6. Answer the question that decides it.** The deciding question isn't technical: what must your team be able to do a year from now that it can't do today? Choose the architecture that makes that possible. Anything beyond that is cost without return.
