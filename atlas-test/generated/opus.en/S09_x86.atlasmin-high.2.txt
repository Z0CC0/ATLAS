x86 grew from a 16-bit chip into a compatible instruction set that sits on top of very different machinery.

**8086 (1978):** 16 bit. Grew out of the 8080 and stayed compatible with it at source level.

**80286 (1982):** Introduced protected mode. There was no way back to real mode without a reboot.

**80386 (1985):** Brought 32 bit, paged virtual memory and virtual 8086 mode.

**486 (1989):** Put the first-level cache and the maths coprocessor on the chip.

**Pentium (1993):** Superscalar. Two integer pipelines could retire two instructions per cycle.

**Pentium Pro (1995):** Added out-of-order execution and translated x86 instructions into internal micro-operations. From here on, x86 is an interface and no longer the real machine underneath.

**Pentium 4 (2000):** Chased frequency with a very long pipeline and hit the power wall.

**AMD64 (2003):** AMD extended the architecture to 64 bit, and Intel adopted the same instruction set. It was the first time the dominant extension did not come from Intel.

**Core 2 (2006):** The focus moved from frequency to core count.

**Across generations:** The vector extensions kept growing: MMX, SSE, AVX, AVX-512.

**Today:** The complexity sits in the layers below: branch prediction, the cache hierarchy and speculative execution.

**Spectre and Meltdown (2018):** Showed that speculative execution carried a security cost nobody had priced.
