First, list which parts of the monolith actually change, and which parts share a transaction. Those two lists should drive the architecture more than any pattern will.

## 1. Map what changes
Parts of the monolith that stay stable gain nothing from being split out. Put your effort only where change actually happens.

## 2. Map the transaction boundaries
Find where your transactions start and end. A transaction that spans two services becomes a distributed problem. If two pieces must commit together, draw the boundary around both of them, not between them.

## 3. Don't do a big-bang rewrite
A full rewrite that runs in parallel with the old system almost always fails. The old system keeps changing and the copy never catches up.

Strangle the monolith gradually instead:
1. Put a router in front of the monolith.
2. Move one piece at a time behind it.
3. Keep the old system authoritative until you've verified the new piece.

## 4. Price in the cost of running microservices
Every service needs its own releases, monitoring, logs, alerts and a person who looks after it. With fewer than five developers, that cost almost always exceeds the benefit.

## 5. Consider the modular monolith
This is the option nobody mentions. You get sharp internal boundaries and a single release, and you can still extract a module the day you genuinely need to. It works well with steps 1 and 2: the modules follow the parts that change and the transaction boundaries.

## The question that decides it
The deciding question isn't technical: **what must your team be able to do a year from now that it can't do today?** Choose the architecture that makes that possible, and nothing more.
