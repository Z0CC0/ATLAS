Start from one question, and it is not technical: what must your team be able to do a year from now that it cannot do today? Judge every architecture choice against that answer.

**Before picking an architecture, map the monolith:**
- **Which parts actually change.** Stable parts gain nothing from being split, so leave them alone.
- **Where the transaction boundaries sit.** A transaction that spans two services becomes a distributed problem. Draw your boundaries so they do not cut through a transaction.

**How to migrate:**
- Avoid a full rewrite running in parallel with the old system. That approach almost always fails because the old system keeps changing and the copy never catches up.
- Strangle the monolith progressively instead:
  1. Put a router in front of the monolith.
  2. Move one piece at a time.
  3. Keep the old system authoritative until the new piece is verified.

**Count the cost of microservices:**
- Microservices cost more to operate. Every service needs its own releases, monitoring, logs and alerts, plus a person who looks after it.
- With fewer than five developers, that cost almost always exceeds the benefit.

**Consider the option nobody mentions: a modular monolith.**
- It has sharp internal boundaries and ships as one release.
- You keep the option to extract a module the day you genuinely need to.
- For a small team, or when only a few parts change, this is probably your best starting point.

To get from these facts to a decision:
1. Answer the one-year question.
2. Find the parts that change and the transaction boundaries.
3. Start with a modular monolith.
4. Extract a service only when a real need appears, using the router and one piece at a time.
