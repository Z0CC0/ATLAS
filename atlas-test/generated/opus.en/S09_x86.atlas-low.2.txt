x86 started as a 16-bit chip that grew out of the 8080. Over the decades it turned into a stable instruction set, and the real machine now sits underneath it.

**Foundations (1978–1989).** The 8086 of 1978 was 16-bit and compatible with the 8080 at source level, so existing code could be carried over by recompiling. The 80286 of 1982 added protected mode, with one awkward limit: once in protected mode, the only way back to real mode was a reboot. The 80386 of 1985 fixed most of that. It brought 32-bit operation, paged virtual memory, and virtual 8086 mode, which let old real-mode programs run inside the protected environment. The 486 of 1989 moved the first-level cache and the maths coprocessor onto the chip itself.

**Parallelism inside the chip (1993–1995).** The Pentium of 1993 was superscalar: it had two integer pipelines and could retire two instructions per cycle. The Pentium Pro of 1995 made the bigger break. It executed instructions out of order and translated x86 instructions into internal micro-operations. From then on, x86 was an interface rather than the real machine underneath.

**Frequency hits the power wall (2000).** The Pentium 4 of 2000 chased clock speed with a very long pipeline. It ran into the power wall: higher clock speeds could no longer be delivered within practical power limits.

**64 bit comes from AMD (2003).** AMD extended the architecture to 64 bit in 2003 with AMD64, and Intel adopted the same instruction set. It was the first time the dominant extension did not come from Intel.

**More cores instead of more clock (2006 on).** With the Core 2 in 2006, the direction shifted from raising frequency to adding cores. Across the same generations the vector extensions kept growing: MMX, then SSE, then AVX, then AVX-512.

**Today.** Most of the complexity now sits in the layers below the instruction set: branch prediction, the cache hierarchy and speculative execution. Spectre and Meltdown in 2018 showed that speculative execution had carried a security cost nobody had priced in.
