HTTPS is ordinary HTTP sent through a channel that TLS encrypts. TLS sets that channel up during a short exchange called the handshake. After the handshake, your HTTP requests and responses travel encrypted inside it.

## The handshake, step by step

1. **Client says hello.** Your browser opens the connection and lists the TLS versions and ciphers (encryption methods) it supports.
2. **Server answers.** It picks one version and one cipher from that list and sends its certificate.
3. **Client checks the certificate.** A certificate binds a domain name to a public key, and a certificate authority (CA) signs it. To check that signature, the browser follows the chain of signatures up to a root certificate it already has stored. If the chain ends at a root it trusts, it accepts the certificate.
4. **Both sides agree a session key.** These days this is usually done with an elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman exchange.
5. **Encrypted traffic starts.** From here on, all traffic is encrypted and authenticated. Anyone sitting between you and the server can't read it or change it.

## Why the session key is symmetric

The certificate uses asymmetric cryptography, where the public and private keys are different. That's useful for proving identity, but it's too slow and costly to run on every byte of real traffic. So the handshake ends with a shared symmetric key, and that cheaper key encrypts the actual data.

## TLS 1.3 vs TLS 1.2

- **TLS 1.2:** the handshake takes two round trips between client and server.
- **TLS 1.3:** it takes one round trip, so the connection is ready sooner.

## What the padlock does not prove

The certificate tells you only that whoever controls that domain also holds that key. It says nothing about who runs the site. A phishing site on a domain it controls can have a perfectly valid certificate. HTTPS means the connection is private and hasn't been tampered with. It does not mean the site is honest.
