Before picking an architecture, answer one question: **what must your team be able to do a year from now that it cannot do today?** That question is not technical, and it decides everything below.

**1. Map what changes.**
Find which parts of the monolith change often. Stable parts gain nothing from being split, so leave them where they are. Put your effort only where change happens.

**2. Map transaction boundaries.**
Find where transactions start and end. A transaction that spans two services becomes a distributed problem. Draw your cuts so each transaction stays inside one unit.

**3. Don't do a parallel full rewrite.**
Rebuilding the whole thing next to the old system almost always fails. The old system keeps changing, and the copy never catches up.

**4. Strangle it step by step instead.**
- Put a router in front of the monolith.
- Move one piece at a time behind the router.
- Keep the old system authoritative until the new piece is verified.

**5. Count the operational cost of microservices.**
Every service needs its own releases, monitoring, logs, alerts, and a person who looks after it. With fewer than five developers, that cost almost always exceeds the benefit.

**6. Consider a modular monolith.**
It's the option nobody mentions. You get sharp internal boundaries and one release. You can still extract a module the day you genuinely need to. For a small team it's often the right target. It also works well as the first step of the strangling approach.

**Suggested order:** first define your one-year goal. Then map where change happens and where transactions sit. Choose a modular monolith or services based on team size and goal. Migrate piece by piece behind the router, never as a big-bang rewrite.
