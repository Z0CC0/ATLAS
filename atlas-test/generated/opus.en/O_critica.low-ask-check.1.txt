I wouldn't put all of it there. Keep integrity rules in the database and business logic in the application, behind one entry point.

You're right about the upside. No application path can skip a trigger, not even a script someone runs by hand.

The costs:

Triggers can't be seen by anyone reading the application code. A change that looks simple can fire other triggers and leave no trace.

Your team's usual test tools don't cover them. You need tests that run against a real database.

Debugging is harder. When a trigger fails, you just get a failed write, with nothing saying which rule caused it.

Migrations become fragile. Trigger order matters, and nothing declares it.

**Alternative:** keep in the database only what protects integrity: constraints, foreign keys, uniqueness and domain checks. Put the business logic in the application, behind a single entry point that every write goes through.

That entry point gives you the same guarantee as a trigger, **as long as direct database access is reserved for migrations**. Without that condition, someone can go around it and you lose the guarantee.
