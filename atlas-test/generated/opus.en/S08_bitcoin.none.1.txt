Bitcoin grew out of the cypherpunk scene, a community that had been trying for years to build digital money that no institution controlled. Earlier proposals such as b-money and bit gold got part of the way, but none of them solved one key problem: getting everyone to agree on the order in which transactions happened.

**The paper**

In 2008 a paper appeared called *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*. It was signed Satoshi Nakamoto, a pseudonym whose owner has never been identified. The paper took on the problem of double spending. Digital money can be copied, so the question is how to stop someone spending the same coin twice when no central body keeps the ledger.

Nakamoto's answer was a chain of blocks ordered by a proof of work. Each block of transactions sits on top of the one before it, and adding a block takes real computational effort. That makes rewriting the past expensive. To change an old transaction, someone would have to redo the proof of work for that block and for every block after it. The longer the chain gets, the harder that becomes.

**The launch**

The first block was generated on 3 January 2009. It contains the headline of a Times article about a bank bailout. The reference does two jobs. It proves the block couldn't have been made before that date, and it says what the project was reacting against.

The rules for issuing new coins were built in from the start. Whoever adds a block receives a reward. That reward started at 50 bitcoin and halves every 210,000 blocks, which works out to roughly every four years. Because of the halvings, the total number of bitcoin tends toward 21 million and can never go past it.

**First use and Satoshi's exit**

In 2010 came the first documented commercial transaction: two pizzas bought for 10,000 bitcoin. In 2011 Satoshi stopped taking part publicly. The bitcoin attributed to Satoshi have never been moved, which adds to the mystery around who created the system.
