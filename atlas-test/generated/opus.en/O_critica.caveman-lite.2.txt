I wouldn't put all of it there. Your goal is sound, though. Triggers do give one real guarantee: no application path can skip the rule, not even a script someone runs by hand.

Moving all business logic into triggers has costs that build up over time:

- **Hidden behavior.** Someone reading the application code can't see triggers. A change that looks simple can fire other changes and leave no trace of them.
- **Testing.** Your team's current test tools won't cover triggers. You would need tests that run against a real database.
- **Debugging.** When a trigger raises an error, it shows up only as a failed write. Nothing tells you which rule caused it.
- **Migrations.** Trigger order matters, and nothing declares that order. Migrations become fragile as a result.

I recommend splitting the work by job:

- **Keep integrity rules in the database:** constraints, foreign keys, uniqueness and domain checks.
- **Keep business logic in the application,** behind a single entry point that every write goes through.

That single entry point gives you the same guarantee as a trigger, with one condition: only migrations may access the database directly. Enforce that rule and nothing can bypass your business rules. They also stay visible, testable with your current tools, and easier to debug.
