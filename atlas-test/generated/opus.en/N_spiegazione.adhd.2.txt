**Eventual consistency means that after you write data, your copies of it (replicas) don't agree right away. If the writes stop, they converge within a finite time.** You should care because it decides what your users see right after they change something.

## What it looks like in practice

A user posts a comment and reloads the page. The comment isn't there. The write reached one replica, and the reload read from another replica that hasn't caught up yet.

How long the gap lasts:
- **Replicas in the same region:** usually under a second
- **Replicas across regions:** it rises to seconds

The effect is short, but it lands on exactly the person who just acted, and they notice.

## Why systems accept it

You're trading consistency against availability. A system that demands strong consistency has to **refuse requests** when its replicas can't talk to each other. An eventually consistent system keeps answering and catches up later.

So this isn't a defect to eliminate. It's a choice, and most systems that scale make it deliberately.

## The usual fix

Send reads to the primary replica **for the session that just wrote**, not for everyone.
- The person who posted the comment sees it immediately.
- Everyone else keeps reading from the replicas and gets the comment within a second or a few seconds.
- The load stays spread out, because only a small share of reads goes to the primary.

## When it's not acceptable

There are only a few such cases, and they're easy to recognise:
1. **Account balances**
2. **Seat availability**
3. **Uniqueness of an identifier**

In all three, two replicas that briefly disagree can produce a real error, such as the same seat sold twice or the same ID given out twice. Those need strong consistency. Everything else can usually live with a lag of about a second.
