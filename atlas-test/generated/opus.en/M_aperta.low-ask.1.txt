Start with what your team must be able to do a year from now that it can't do today. That question decides the architecture. The technical questions come after it.

Next, find out which parts of the monolith actually change. Stable parts gain nothing from being split out, so leave them alone.

Then map where the transaction boundaries sit. A transaction that spans two services becomes a distributed problem. Wherever you draw a service line, try not to cut through one.

Don't rewrite the whole thing in parallel with the old system. That approach almost always fails: the old system keeps changing and the copy never catches up.

Strangle it progressively instead. Put a router in front, move one piece at a time, and keep the PHP system authoritative until each new piece is verified.

Count the operational cost of microservices honestly. Every service needs releases, monitoring, logs, alerts and a person who looks after it. With fewer than five developers, that cost almost always exceeds the benefit.

Also consider the **modular monolith**, the option people rarely mention. It gives you sharp internal boundaries and a single release, and you can still extract a module the day you genuinely need to. For a small team, or when you're unsure where the boundaries are, it's usually the safer target.
