Eventual consistency describes what happens when your data is stored on several replicas. After a write, the replicas don't all agree right away. If writes stop, they will converge on the same value within a finite time. For a while, different replicas can give different answers to the same question, but they settle.

**Why systems work this way**

It's a trade against availability. Suppose the replicas lose contact with each other. A system that demands strong consistency has no way to confirm they agree, so it has to refuse requests until they can talk again. An eventually consistent system keeps serving requests and reconciles afterwards. Most systems that scale accept this on purpose. Eventual consistency isn't a defect you need to eliminate. It's a choice.

**Why you should care**

It affects anyone who reads right after writing. A typical case: a user posts a comment, reloads the page, and doesn't see it. Their read reached a replica that hadn't received the write yet. Nothing was lost, but to the user it looks broken.

The gap is usually short. Replicas in the same region typically converge in under a second. Across regions it can take seconds. That's short enough that most people never notice, and long enough that the post-then-reload case does happen.

**The common fix**

For the session that just made a write, send its reads to the primary replica. Everyone else keeps reading from the other replicas. The person who posted the comment sees it immediately, and you don't give up the scaling benefits for all other traffic.

**When it's not acceptable**

The cases are few and easy to recognise:
- **Account balances:** you can't let two replicas each approve spending the same money.
- **Seat availability:** you can't sell the same seat twice.
- **Uniqueness of an identifier:** two users can't both claim the same username or ID.

For these, pay the cost of strong consistency. For almost everything else, eventual consistency is a reasonable default. Just plan for the read-your-own-write case.
