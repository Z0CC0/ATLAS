Start with Postgres alone. It handles orders, catalogue, text search and analytics acceptably, though none of them outstandingly. It also gives you real transactions, and on orders you can't do without them.

Search and analytics are Postgres's limits. Built-in text search is good enough below a million documents and weak above that. Analytics holds up to tens of millions of rows, then the scans get slow.

MySQL is equivalent on orders but weaker on data types and complex queries. There's no reason to pick it over Postgres.

MongoDB makes the catalogue easier because it allows variable schemas. But multi-document transactions arrived late and they're costly, which is a problem on orders.

Elasticsearch is far better at text search: relevance, synonyms and tolerance of typos. It's not an authoritative store, so it has to be fed from another database, not replace it. Adding it below a million documents is almost always premature.

ClickHouse is far better at analytics, with columnar scans orders of magnitude faster. It's not for orders because point updates are its weak spot.

If you grow, the usual setup is Postgres as the authoritative store, with Elasticsearch and ClickHouse fed from it. Each system you add brings a synchronisation problem and a data alignment problem. Add one only when a specific workload demands it and you have numbers to show it.

The real cost of specialised systems isn't the licence. It's the time of the people who keep them running.
