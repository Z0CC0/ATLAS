# Explaining a decision

The reader wants to know whether to trust the choice, or whether to reopen it. Both need
the same thing: the options that were on the table, what each would have cost, what decided
it, and what would make it wrong.

## Shape

**The question** as it was asked at the time, in one sentence, with the date and who
asked. A decision explained without its question looks arbitrary.

**The constraint that decided it**, before the options: the thing that could not move
(one machine, no network, a deadline, a rule of the house). Most decisions are made by one
constraint; name it first and the options explain themselves.

**The options**, two to four, each in the same shape: what it is, what it would have given,
what it would have cost, why it lost. The option chosen is written in the same shape as the
others, with what it cost too. An option that was never real ("rewrite everything") is not
listed; an option the reader will think of is, even if it was dismissed in a minute, with the
minute's reason.

**What was chosen**, in one sentence, and where it lives now (the file, the setting, the
record).

**What would reopen it**: the fact that, if it changed, would make another option win. This
is the most useful line for a reader six months later, and the one most often missing.

## Sources

Every claim about what was considered comes from a record: the decision file, the commit
message, the conversation, the memory note. When the record is missing, the explanation
says "reconstructed from the code as it is; the reasoning is inferred", so the reader does
not mistake a guess for a memory.

## What it is not

Not an argument for the decision: the reader may disagree, and a one-sided explanation
hides what they need to disagree with. Not a history of everything tried. Not a place to
change the decision: if writing the explanation shows the decision is now wrong, say so at
the end, as a separate finding, and leave the explanation true to what was decided.
